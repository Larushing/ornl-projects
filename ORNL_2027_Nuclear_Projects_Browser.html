Designing Secure and Safer Nuclear Facility Sites with Geospatial Analysis

Abiodun Adeniyi — Nuclear Science and Engineering Division · Nuclear Infrastructure Deployment and Op

Open to: U.S. citizens only

My work focuses on improving how potential sites for advanced reactors, small modular reactors, and other nuclear facilities are evaluated before major design and location decisions are made. The project combines geographic information systems (GIS), nuclear siting criteria, security-informed analysis, and multi-criteria decision methods. As an intern, you could help identify site characteristics that affect safety, physical protection, emergency response, transportation access, infrastructure, and community compatibility. Depending on your background, you may organize geospatial data, develop screening criteria, compare candidate locations, test weighting approaches, or create maps and dashboards that communicate results. You may also contribute to the continued development and application of the Reactor Impact, Siting, and Security Evaluation (RISSE) framework and related ORNL siting tools. The work offers practical experience at the intersection of nuclear engineering, national security, data analysis, and geospatial decision support for DOE missions.

Assessment of thermal and radiation induced creep in the dual cooled lead lithium blanket

Sunday Aduloju — Fusion Science and Technology Division · Blanket and Fuel Cycle

Open to: Open to all, including foreign nationals

The creep in recently designed blanket for the fusion nuclear science facility would be described by decomposing it into thermal, irradiation and cavity swelling creep. Initial estimates of thermal creep using elastic high temperature rules showed that the recent heat transfer improvements at the first wall (FW) is feasible, and the FW can withstand heat flux of 0.35 MW/m2 at 3000 hrs without rupture. A viscoplastic model that is based on Norton’s power law and relies on Multiphysics coupling of solid mechanics and heat transfer modules is would be used to capture the inelastic deformation in the blanket. The maximum thermal creep strain and the relaxation of the thermal stress at the FW at the peak heat loads would be calculated. Irradiation creep would be prescribed to be proportional to the displacement damage dose and applied stress. The displacements from irradiation creep and the cavity swelling creep at the first wall and back wall of the blanket would be studied.

Research area: Fusion Energy and Plasma Science; Mechanical and Structural Engineering

Methods and tools: Computational engineering and mechanics (CFD, finite element analysis, etc.)

Recommended majors: Mechanical / Industrial Engineering; Materials Science and Engineering; Nuclear Engineering

Powder rejuvenation and hot isostatic pressing of steels and nickel-based alloys

Pavan Ajjarapu — Materials Science and Technology Div · Material Processing

Open to: Open to all, including foreign nationals

Energy abundance demands accelerated development, demonstration, and deployment of advanced manufacturing methods for next-generation nuclear technologies. Powder metallurgical hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) is being considered for fabricating large reactor pressure vessel components, but qualifying PM-HIP SS316 requires mechanistic understanding of how powder feedstock and post-HIP heat-treatment control creep performance. This work will presents an end-to-end workflow to establish processing–microstructure relationships. Multi-scale characterization of SS316 powders revealed major differences in elemental segregation, oxide morphology, secondary phase formation, and thermodynamic stability, underscoring the roles of powder chemistry and processing history. A rotary-kiln powder rejuvenation approach using reducing atmospheres to modify powder surfaces prior to consolidation will be explored. In parallel, systematic post-HIP solution annealing will be quantified to understand competing effects of precipitate dissolution, grain growth, and oxide evolution. Collectively, this work is geared towards strengthening the scientific basis for creep-relevant qualification of PM-HIP SS316 for advanced nuclear deployment.

Research area: Materials Science and Nanotechnology; Mechanical and Structural Engineering; Advanced Manufacturing and 3D Printing; Neutron Scattering and Imaging (diffraction, spectroscopy and tomography using neutron beams)

Methods and tools: Data analysis and statistics; Beamline and large-facility experiments; Mechanical and materials testing; Analytical measurement and characterization (microscopy, spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, etc)

High Performance Thermoelectric Materials and Devices for Nuclear Batteries

Hugo Bouteiller — Materials Science and Technology Div · Applied Microanalysis and Thermophysics

Open to: U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents

My work focuses on the development of high performance thermoelectric materials such as skutterudites for high temperature nuclear battery applications. A nuclear battery is a Radioisotope Power System that provides a reliable source of electrical energy for several years in extreme environments such as the arctics, deep seas or space. The thermal energy provided by the nuclear source is converted into electricity by thermoelectric materials which present advanced features leading to very low thermal conductivity and good electronic transport. These transport properties are correlated, and the scientific community is working hard to decouple them to reach higher performance thermoelectric materials for next generation nuclear batteries. The student will participate in the synthesis, characterization, and implementation of thermoelectric materials into devices to address the most pressing challenges in the field and contribute to impactful outcomes. The student will also be able to leverage the internal AI tool called SCATTER that the research team is actively developing to accelerate research. While all applications are welcome, a graduate student level is expected for this internship.

Fast Reactor Neutronics with MPACT

Andrew Conant — Nuclear Nonproliferation Division · Uranium Chemistry, Application and Simul

Open to: U.S. citizens only

The DOE Consortium for the Advanced Simulation of Light Water Reactors (CASL) was a program that aimed to improve computational models for light water reactors in the 2010s. Since CASL ended in 2020, there has been ongoing effort to leverage the tool for various applications in PWRs and, more recently, BWRs though CASL’s Virtual Environment for Reactor Applications (VERA). The primary neutronics solver in VERA is MPACT, which is a 2D-1D MOC code aimed at solving extruded reactor geometries. Some recent effort has examined effectiveness of using MPACT to model heavy water-moderated and gas-cooled, graphite-moderated reactors. Exploratory effort is needed to examine the effectiveness of MPACT in modeling liquid metal (e.g., sodium) fast reactors. The student on this project will have the opportunity to learn how to create and run various reactor models in MPACT to examine potential utility in fast reactor applications.

Research area: Nuclear Energy and Science; National Security and Nonproliferation

Methods and tools: High performance and scientific computing; Programming and software development; Computational engineering and mechanics (CFD, finite element analysis, etc.)

Recommended majors: Nuclear Engineering; Physics / Astronomy

Required preparation: Nuclear reactor theory, nuclear reactor physics

Gamma-based tomography of nuclear fuel rodlets

Peter Doyle — Nuclear Science and Engineering Division · Nuclear Fuel Element Performance

Open to: Open to all, including foreign nationals

Nuclear fuel rods are frequently examined by gamma spectroscopy to understand the state of the fuel following the life of the fuel in a reactor. This can reveal a variety of characteristics about the fuel such as the location of individual pellets, the hot and cold zones, and the accuracy of burnup calculations. At ORNL, we currently scan full rods to get this information in 1-2 mm axial blocks. However, we are interested in collecting and processing information at several different rod rotations so that we can also perform 2D reconstructions of the specific axial locations on the rod using standard tomography tools. This project will involve collection and processing of the data, and evaluation of the uncertainties and collection requirements for quality reconstructions.

Research area: Nuclear Energy and Science

Methods and tools: Programming and software development; Data analysis and statistics; Analytical measurement and characterization (microscopy, spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, etc)

Data-Driven Warm Starts for Families of Nonlinear Equations

Jonas Katona — Computer Science and Mathematics Div · Computer Science and Mathematics Div

Open to: Open to all, including foreign nationals

Many problems in science and engineering require solving a nonlinear equation many times for different parameter values. Algorithms such as Newton’s method can be very fast when given a good initial guess, but slow or unreliable when initialized poorly. This project asks: if we need to solve many related nonlinear equations, can we intelligently choose a small number to solve first and reuse their solutions as warm starts for the rest? Students will investigate ways to select representative "anchor" problems to solve using ideas from numerical analysis, clustering, sensitivity analysis, and experimental design. Possible tasks include implementing nonlinear solvers, developing curvature-based measures of parameter similarity, and testing whether these methods reduce iteration counts or solver failures compared with simpler choices. The project will primarily involve numerical experiments with opportunities to explore the mathematical theory explaining when and why different warm-start strategies work.

Research area: Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning; High Performance Computing and Data Science; Applied Mathematics and Statistics

Methods and tools: Data analysis and statistics; High performance and scientific computing; Artificial intelligence and machine learning; Computational engineering and mechanics (CFD, finite element analysis, etc.); Programming and software development

Recommended majors: Computer Engineering; Mechanical / Industrial Engineering; Computer Science / Cybersecurity; Data Science / Statistics; Mathematics / Applied Mathematics; Physics / Astronomy; Aerospace Engineering; Chemical Engineering; Nuclear Engineering

Required preparation: Students should have completed foundational coursework in multivariable calculus and linear algebra and should have some prior programming experience, preferably in Python, Julia, or C/C++. The project will involve implementing and testing numerical algorithms, so students should be comfortable writing and debugging mathematical code.

Preferred preparation: Helpful but not required background includes coursework or experience in one or more of the following: numerical analysis, differential equations, numerical optimization, probability or statistics, machine learning, and scientific computing. Resolving Nanoscale Chemical Heterogeneity in Electrochemically grown Passive Films using atom probe tomography

Venkata Bhuvaneswari Vukkum — Center for Nanophase Matls Sciences · electron Microscopy and Microanalysis

Open to: Open to all, including foreign nationals

This project aims to elucidate the nanoscale and atomic-scale chemical architecture of electrochemically formed passive films in two multiphase high-entropy alloys (HEAs), Fe–Cr–Ti–V and Si–Cr–Ti–V. Both alloys exhibit a multiphase microstructure with constituent phases typically <1 μm in size and demonstrate enhanced corrosion resistance during bulk electrochemical testing in 0.6 M NaCl. Electrochemical measurements identify potential regimes associated with the formation and stabilization of protective passive films; however, the atomic-scale chemical characteristics and spatial heterogeneity of these films remain unresolved, particularly with respect to their dependence on the underlying phase chemistry. In this project, passive films will be electrochemically grown under controlled conditions, followed by site-specific preparation of nanoscale APT specimens using electron microscopy and focused ion beam techniques. The prepared specimens will be analyzed using atom probe tomography (APT) to resolve elemental distributions, chemical gradients, and nanoscale heterogeneity across the metal–passive-film interface, enabling direct correlation between phase chemistry and passive-film architecture.

Research area: Materials Science and Nanotechnology; Chemistry: Synthesis, Catalysis and Analysis

Methods and tools: Analytical measurement and characterization (microscopy, spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, etc); Micro- and nanofabrication (cleanroom); Data analysis and statistics; Laboratory chemical synthesis, processing and scale-up
